8.1 What this chapter is for

Every rate in this report is a count of recorded events divided by a layer of population. The counts are described in the source chapters; this chapter describes the layers. None of them is a count of autism, and none of them turns a count of autism into a prevalence: they answer how many people were living in the territory, how many people were insured in December and how many people were enrolled in a primary-care centre, and those are three different questions with three different units.

Two things are set out here. The first is the set of denominators: the INE (national statistics institute) resident population under its two census bases, by region, age group and single year of age, together with the coverage layers of FONASA (the public insurer), ISAPRE (the private insurers) and primary-care enrolment. The second is DEIS (Department of Health Statistics and Information), the national hospital discharge register, which counts the same hospital events as the GRD (Grupos Relacionados por el Diagnóstico) database used in the hospital chapter, but with a different scope, a different diagnostic field and a suppression rule of its own. It is read here as an independent check on those counts, never as a second estimate of how many people are autistic.

Three things run through the chapter and are made visible wherever they bite:

  • The population base changed. INE publishes projections on the 2017 census base and, since 2024, on a 2024 base, and the 2024 census enumerated a smaller country than either projection. A rate computed with one base and compared with a rate computed with another is not a comparison of recognition.
  • Numerator and denominator are often located differently. The health records locate an event where it was recorded — the facility, the hospital — while the population locates a person where they live. Rates built across that difference rank territories; they do not measure the risk of residents.
  • Insurance and primary-care coverage count enrolment, not need. A person enters these layers by an administrative act, at a moment that has nothing to do with their health, and a 31 December stock is not the population of the year.

8.6 Care and residence

The health sources of this report count where care was given. GRD records the hospital; the monthly returns of the public network record the facility; primary-care enrolment records the centre. The population counts where a person lives. Dividing one by the other is legitimate and is what the report does, but it produces a figure about a territory’s activity, not about its residents’ risk:

  • A commune with a referral hospital absorbs episodes from residents of neighbouring communes, and its rate rises for that reason.
  • A commune with no facility reporting a code has no numerator at all, whatever its residents’ contact with the system elsewhere. The community chapter measures how far that goes at commune scale.
  • The public insurer’s own geography is mixed — the commune of primary-care enrolment for those enrolled, the domicile for the rest — so even inside a single coverage layer the territorial unit is not uniform.

The hospital chapter’s territorial rates are the exception that shows the rule: GRD carries the commune of residence, so its numerator and its denominator sit in the same unit. Wherever a rate in this report does not, the figure is labelled a rate by place of care.

8.7 DEIS: an independent count of the same hospital events

8.7.1 What DEIS is, and what it can be compared with

DEIS publishes the national hospital discharge register: every establishment in the country, public and private, one row per discharge. GRD covers the public hospitals that operate the diagnosis-related-group system and records up to thirty-five diagnostic positions per episode. The two registers are not linked, by person or by episode, and they do not measure the same quantity:

8.8 What these sources cannot support

  • No layer here is a denominator for autism. The population layers answer how many people live in a territory; the coverage layers answer how many were enrolled on 31 December. Dividing a count of recognised autism by any of them gives a rate of recorded events per 100 000 population — never a prevalence, never an incidence, never a share of the people who have autism.
  • The bases are not interchangeable. A rate computed on the 2017 base and a rate computed on the 2024 base differ by up to 1.48% for the same records, the widest gap falling in 2021; against the 2024 census enumeration the difference is about 8.7%. No series in this report crosses that break.
  • The spans differ. The regional population runs 2017–2024, the coverage layers 2019–2025 and the DEIS comparison 2019–2024. Any indicator combining two of them stops where the shorter one stops.
  • The stocks are December; the population is 30 June. Every coverage percentage in this chapter mixes those two dates. It is indicative, and it is not an insurance-coverage rate.
  • The geographies do not match. Residence, domicile, commune of primary-care enrolment, administrative commune of the beneficiary and commune of the facility are five different things that appear in these tables under regional headings. Regional percentages that mix them rank regions; they do not measure them.
  • One published breakdown does not add back. The region and sex rows of the population layer in the extended tables sum to twice their own national total. The consolidated regional table is used instead, and the discrepancy is stated rather than silently corrected.
  • DEIS sees the principal position only, so it corroborates only that part. Its second diagnostic field is the external cause of injury, so the register cannot reproduce anything the hospital chapter reports about autism documented alongside another reason for admission. In 2024 that is 95.5% of the GRD any-position numerator: the external check reaches the remaining 4.5%. The secondary-position component, which multiplies by 3.96 across the period, is corroborated by nothing in this report.
  • DEIS masks rows, and masks different things in different years. Between 1.7% and 7.9% of discharges per year carry no establishment and no sex, and they contain records with principal F84, so every split by affiliation and by sex is a split of an incomplete file. Their age is masked in 2019–2023 but not in 2024, where the age bands add to the year total exactly; the age split the chapter draws is therefore complete, and the earlier ones are not. The file layout itself changes between years.
  • The GRD panel is not fixed. GRD observes 65 hospitals at the start of the comparison and 72 at the end, the first new ones entering in 2023. On the fixed 65-hospital panel the 2024 any-position count is lower by 251 episodes, so a part of the growth of the series this report headlines is coverage rather than coding. The published series is the observed panel.
  • The coverage layers lose their age-not-reported records. The published age breakdown of the insurance and primary-care stocks omits the records with no age, so it falls short of the December stock of the same layer by up to 0.08% and every coverage-by-age percentage in this chapter is understated by that margin.
  • The primary-care layer’s reporting base grows with it. Centres with enrolled persons rise 10.6% across the period while enrolment rises 14.6% and the population 5.8%, and FONASA’s own count of beneficiaries enrolled in primary care — the only available cross-check — stops in 2022.
  • The DEIS age cells are small. Above 40 years the annual counts of principal F84 are in single digits and their exact intervals span an order of magnitude. Those cells are reported as counts.
  • Neither register is linked to the other. No ratio between DEIS and GRD in this chapter is a probability, a completeness estimate or a capture–recapture quantity. They are ratios of records produced by two offices from two files.

9 Synthesis

  • The study divides by one primary denominator throughout: the INE resident population on the 2017 census base at 30 June, which reaches 20,206,953 persons in the last published year. The 2024-base projection differs from it by at most 1.48% in any year and the 2024 census enumeration by about 8.0%; splicing bases inside one series would move a rate by up to 8.7% with no change in the records at all.
  • Every region gains population over the published span and none loses it, but the speeds differ widely: the country grows 9.1% while the regions run from 3.0% to 18.8% — the fastest 2.1 times the national rate, the slowest 0.33 times it, and 11 of the 16 below the national rate. Territorial counts and territorial rates therefore tell different stories.
  • Insurance and primary-care layers are December stocks of enrolment. The public insurer’s share of the population rises by about 7.1 percentage points across the period while the private insurers’ share falls by about 5.5, and in the youngest age band the two together reach only about 82% of the resident population against about 104% in the band where they exceed it. None of this measures need.
  • DEIS, an independent national register with wider scope and a narrower diagnostic field, records the same rise as GRD in the principal position: the share of recorded hospital activity with autism as the principal diagnosis goes from 18.2 to 40.7 per 100 000 discharges. The corroboration stops there. It covers the 4.5% of the GRD any-position numerator that sits in the principal field; the other 95.5% is invisible to DEIS, and the any-position count that the report headlines multiplies by 3.74 against 1.71 for the corroborated part. The two registers are also drifting apart rather than converging: their principal-count ratio moves from 1.33 to 1.74, and on the public-hospital subset from 1.026 to 1.490. The agreement is about recognition in records, not about how many people are autistic.
  • The two registers disagree about magnitude for a structural reason: DEIS counts 678 principal-position discharges nationally in 2024 against 8,731 public-hospital episodes with an F84 code in any position. Reading only the principal field hides most of what the hospital chapter describes.
  • Between 1.7% and 7.9% of DEIS discharges are published with establishment and sex masked — and, in 2019–2023, age as well — and the public network carries about 86% of the principal-F84 records against about 60% of all discharges — the gap that bounds what a public-hospital database can say about the country.

## What this chapter is for

Every rate in this report is a count of recorded events divided by a layer of population. The counts are
described in the source chapters; this chapter describes the layers. None of them is a count of autism, and
none of them turns a count of autism into a prevalence: they answer *how many people were living in the
territory*, *how many people were insured in December* and *how many people were enrolled in a primary-care
centre*, and those are three different questions with three different units.

Two things are set out here. The first is the set of denominators: the INE (national statistics institute)
resident population under its two census bases, by region, age group and single year of age, together with
the coverage layers of FONASA (the public insurer), ISAPRE (the private insurers) and primary-care
enrolment. The second is DEIS (Department of Health Statistics and Information), the national hospital
discharge register, which counts the same hospital events as the GRD (Grupos Relacionados por el
Diagnóstico) database used in the [hospital chapter](hospital.html), but with a different scope, a different
diagnostic field and a suppression rule of its own. It is read here as an independent check on those counts,
never as a second estimate of how many people are autistic.

Three things run through the chapter and are made visible wherever they bite:

- **The population base changed.** INE publishes projections on the 2017 census base and, since 2024, on a
  2024 base, and the 2024 census enumerated a smaller country than either projection. A rate computed with
  one base and compared with a rate computed with another is not a comparison of recognition.
- **Numerator and denominator are often located differently.** The health records locate an event where it
  was recorded — the facility, the hospital — while the population locates a person where they live. Rates
  built across that difference rank territories; they do not measure the risk of residents.
- **Insurance and primary-care coverage count enrolment, not need.** A person enters these layers by an
  administrative act, at a moment that has nothing to do with their health, and a 31 December stock is not
  the population of the year.

## Care and residence

The health sources of this report count where care was given. GRD records the hospital; the monthly returns
of the public network record the facility; primary-care enrolment records the centre. The population counts
where a person lives. Dividing one by the other is legitimate and is what the report does, but it produces a
figure about a territory's activity, not about its residents' risk:

- A commune with a referral hospital absorbs episodes from residents of neighbouring communes, and its rate
  rises for that reason.
- A commune with no facility reporting a code has no numerator at all, whatever its residents' contact with
  the system elsewhere. The [community chapter](community.html) measures how far that goes at commune scale.
- The public insurer's own geography is mixed — the commune of primary-care enrolment for those enrolled,
  the domicile for the rest — so even inside a single coverage layer the territorial unit is not uniform.

The hospital chapter's territorial rates are the exception that shows the rule: GRD carries the commune of
residence, so its numerator and its denominator sit in the same unit. Wherever a rate in this report does
not, the figure is labelled a rate by place of care.

## DEIS: an independent count of the same hospital events

### What DEIS is, and what it can be compared with

DEIS publishes the national hospital discharge register: every establishment in the country, public and
private, one row per discharge. GRD covers the public hospitals that operate the diagnosis-related-group
system and records up to thirty-five diagnostic positions per episode. The two registers are not linked, by
person or by episode, and they do not measure the same quantity:

| | DEIS discharge register | GRD episode database |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | All establishments in the country | Public hospitals operating GRD, a panel that grows mid-series |
| Unit | Hospital discharge | Episode, including day surgery and other modalities |
| Diagnostic fields read | The principal diagnosis; the second field is the external cause | Up to thirty-five positions |
| Autism visible in | The principal position only | Any position |
| Suppression | Rows with establishment and sex masked, and in most years their age as well | No masking; identifier audit published separately |

Exactly one series is therefore comparable: **F84 as the principal diagnosis**. DEIS has no equivalent of a
secondary-position diagnosis, because its second field is reserved for the external cause of injury — across
the six published years the count of F84 in that field is
`{python} number(f84_external_cause)`. Everything the hospital chapter says about autism documented
alongside another reason for admission is invisible in DEIS by construction.

`{python} number(growth_grd_principal, 2)`. Two registers built by different offices, from different files,
with different scope, record the same rise **in the principal position**. That is the strongest available
evidence that the rise in principal-position autism coding in GRD is not an artefact of the GRD extraction.

It is not evidence about the series the report headlines. That series counts F84 in any of thirty-five
positions, and in `{python} str(last_deis_year)`
`{python} number(last_deis.grd_f84_secondary_only)` of its
`{python} number(last_deis.grd_f84_any)` episodes —
`{python} number(secondary_share_last, 1)`%, up from
`{python} number(secondary_share_first, 1)`% in `{python} str(first_deis_year)` — carry the code in a

numerator is the `{python} number(corroborated_share_last, 1)`% that sits in the principal field. The
secondary-position component multiplies by `{python} number(growth_grd_secondary, 2)` over the same six

corroboration whatever. The headline any-position count multiplies by
`{python} number(growth_grd_any, 2)` and the published crude rate by `{python} number(consistent, 2)`;
neither of those multipliers has been checked against a second register, and nothing in this section can
check them.

The agreement also drifts. The ratio of the two principal counts does not sit still inside its range: it
moves from `{python} number(deis.ratio_deis_f84_principal_to_grd_f84_principal.iloc[0], 2)` in
`{python} str(first_deis_year)` to
`{python} number(deis.ratio_deis_f84_principal_to_grd_f84_principal.iloc[-1], 2)` in
`{python} str(last_deis_year)`, and the tighter comparison — DEIS's own public-hospital subset against GRD,

`{python} number(snss_ratio.iloc[0], 3)` to `{python} number(snss_ratio.iloc[-1], 3)`, a divergence of
`{python} number(snss_drift, 0)`%. Two registers that counted the same public-hospital discharges to within
`{python} number(100 * (snss_ratio.iloc[0] - 1), 1)`% of each other at the start of the period differ by
`{python} number(100 * (snss_ratio.iloc[-1] - 1), 1)`% at the end. Whatever the two registers agree about,
they are not agreeing more closely over time, and part of the GRD side of that drift is the panel growing
from `{python} str(panel_first)` hospitals to `{python} str(panel_last)`.

The instructive part is the size of the numbers. DEIS counts
`{python} number(deis_last.deis_f84_principal)` discharges in the whole country in
`{python} str(last_deis_year)` with autism as the principal diagnosis, while GRD counts
`{python} number(deis_last.grd_f84_any)` public-hospital episodes with an F84 code somewhere in the record.

the principal field cannot see that. Neither number is a count of autistic people, and the ratio between
them — `{python} number(deis_last.ratio_grd_f84_any_to_deis_f84_principal, 1)` in the last year — is a ratio
of records in two different registers, not a multiplier to apply to anything.

## What these sources cannot support

- **No layer here is a denominator for autism.** The population layers answer how many people live in a
  territory; the coverage layers answer how many were enrolled on 31 December. Dividing a count of
  recognised autism by any of them gives a rate of recorded events per 100 000 population — never a
  prevalence, never an incidence, never a share of the people who have autism.
- **The bases are not interchangeable.** A rate computed on the 2017 base and a rate computed on the 2024
  base differ by up to `{python} number(base_gap_max, 2)`% for the same records, the widest gap falling in
  `{python} str(base_gap_year)`; against the 2024 census enumeration the difference is about
  `{python} number(100 * (1 / census_ratio - 1), 1)`%. No series in this report crosses that break.
- **The spans differ.** The regional population runs
  `{python} f"{first_population_year}–{last_population_year}"`, the coverage layers
  `{python} f"{first_coverage_year}–{last_coverage_year}"` and the DEIS comparison
  `{python} f"{first_deis_year}–{last_deis_year}"`. Any indicator combining two of them stops where the
  shorter one stops.
- **The stocks are December; the population is 30 June.** Every coverage percentage in this chapter mixes
  those two dates. It is indicative, and it is not an insurance-coverage rate.
- **The geographies do not match.** Residence, domicile, commune of primary-care enrolment, administrative
  commune of the beneficiary and commune of the facility are five different things that appear in these
  tables under regional headings. Regional percentages that mix them rank regions; they do not measure them.
- **One published breakdown does not add back.** The region and sex rows of the population layer in the
  extended tables sum to twice their own national total. The consolidated regional table is used instead,
  and the discrepancy is stated rather than silently corrected.
- **DEIS sees the principal position only, so it corroborates only that part.** Its second diagnostic field
  is the external cause of injury, so the register cannot reproduce anything the hospital chapter reports
  about autism documented alongside another reason for admission. In
  `{python} str(last_deis_year)` that is `{python} number(secondary_share_last, 1)`% of the GRD any-position
  numerator: the external check reaches the remaining
  `{python} number(corroborated_share_last, 1)`%. The secondary-position component, which multiplies by
  `{python} number(growth_grd_secondary, 2)` across the period, is corroborated by nothing in this report.
- **DEIS masks rows, and masks different things in different years.** Between
  `{python} number((100 * masking["Discharges with masked affiliation"] / masking["DEIS discharges (all establishments)"]).min(), 1)`%
  and
  `{python} number((100 * masking["Discharges with masked affiliation"] / masking["DEIS discharges (all establishments)"]).max(), 1)`%
  of discharges per year carry no establishment and no sex, and they contain records with principal F84, so
  every split by affiliation and by sex is a split of an incomplete file. Their age is masked in
  `{python} year_span(age_masked_years)` but not in
  `{python} year_span(age_complete_years)`, where the age bands add to the year total
  exactly; the age split the chapter draws is therefore complete, and the earlier ones are not. The file
  layout itself changes between years.
- **The GRD panel is not fixed.** GRD observes `{python} str(panel_first)` hospitals at the start of the
  comparison and `{python} str(panel_last)` at the end, the first new ones entering
  in `{python} str(panel_break)`. On the fixed `{python} str(panel_first)`-hospital panel the
  `{python} str(last_deis_year)` any-position count is lower by `{python} number(any_gap)` episodes, so a
  part of the growth of the series this report headlines is coverage rather than coding. The published
  series is the observed panel.
- **The coverage layers lose their age-not-reported records.** The published age breakdown of the insurance
  and primary-care stocks omits the records with no age, so it falls short of the December stock of the same
  layer by up to `{python} number(worst_shortfall["% of the layer"], 2)`% and every coverage-by-age
  percentage in this chapter is understated by that margin.
- **The primary-care layer's reporting base grows with it.** Centres with enrolled persons rise
  `{python} number(centres_change, 1)`% across the period while enrolment rises
  `{python} number(aps_change, 1)`% and the population `{python} number(population_change, 1)`%, and
  FONASA's own count of beneficiaries enrolled in primary care — the only available cross-check — stops
  in `{python} str(aps_crosscheck_last)`.
- **The DEIS age cells are small.** Above 40 years the annual counts of principal F84 are in single digits
  and their exact intervals span an order of magnitude. Those cells are reported as counts.
- **Neither register is linked to the other.** No ratio between DEIS and GRD in this chapter is a
  probability, a completeness estimate or a capture–recapture quantity. They are ratios of records produced
  by two offices from two files.

# Synthesis

- The study divides by one primary denominator throughout: the INE resident population on the 2017 census
  base at 30 June, which reaches `{python} number(coverage_last.ine_population_base2017_30jun)` persons in
  the last published year. The 2024-base projection differs from it by at most
  `{python} number(base_gap_max, 2)`% in any year and the
  2024 census enumeration by about `{python} number(100 * (1 - census_ratio), 1)`%; splicing bases inside
  one series would move a rate by up to
  `{python} number(100 * (1 / census_ratio - 1), 1)`% with no change in the records at all.
- Every region gains population over the published span and none loses it, but the speeds differ widely:
  the country grows `{python} number(national_change, 1)`% while the regions run from
  `{python} number(region_change.min(), 1)`% to `{python} number(region_change.max(), 1)`% — the fastest
  `{python} number(region_change.max() / national_change, 1)` times the national rate, the slowest
  `{python} number(region_change.min() / national_change, 2)` times it, and
  `{python} str(slower_regions)` of the `{python} str(len(REGION_ORDER))` below the national rate. Territorial
  counts and territorial rates therefore tell different stories.
- Insurance and primary-care layers are December stocks of enrolment. The public insurer's share of the
  population rises by about
  `{python} number(100 * (coverage.share_fonasa_ine.iloc[-1] - coverage.share_fonasa_ine.iloc[0]), 1)`
  percentage points across the period while the private insurers' share falls by about
  `{python} number(100 * (coverage.share_isapre_ine.iloc[0] - coverage.share_isapre_ine.iloc[-1]), 1)`, and
  in the youngest age band the two together reach only about
  `{python} number(youngest_band["(FONASA + ISAPRE) ÷ INE (%)"], 0)`% of the resident population against
  about `{python} number(widest_band["(FONASA + ISAPRE) ÷ INE (%)"], 0)`% in the band where they exceed it.
  None of this measures need.
- DEIS, an independent national register with wider scope and a narrower diagnostic field, records the same
  rise as GRD **in the principal position**: the share of recorded hospital activity with autism as the
  principal diagnosis goes from
  `{python} number(deis.deis_rate_f84_principal_per_100k_discharges.iloc[0], 1)` to
  `{python} number(deis.deis_rate_f84_principal_per_100k_discharges.iloc[-1], 1)` per 100 000 discharges.
  The corroboration stops there. It covers the
  `{python} number(corroborated_share_last, 1)`% of the GRD any-position numerator that sits in the
  principal field; the other `{python} number(secondary_share_last, 1)`% is invisible to DEIS, and the
  any-position count that the report headlines multiplies by
  `{python} number(growth_grd_any, 2)` against `{python} number(growth_grd_principal, 2)` for the
  corroborated part. The two registers are also drifting apart rather than converging: their principal-count
  ratio moves from `{python} number(deis.ratio_deis_f84_principal_to_grd_f84_principal.iloc[0], 2)` to
  `{python} number(deis.ratio_deis_f84_principal_to_grd_f84_principal.iloc[-1], 2)`, and on the
  public-hospital subset from `{python} number(snss_ratio.iloc[0], 3)` to
  `{python} number(snss_ratio.iloc[-1], 3)`. The agreement is about recognition in records, not about how
  many people are autistic.
- The two registers disagree about magnitude for a structural reason: DEIS counts
  `{python} number(deis_last.deis_f84_principal)` principal-position discharges nationally in
  `{python} str(last_deis_year)` against `{python} number(deis_last.grd_f84_any)` public-hospital episodes
  with an F84 code in any position. Reading only the principal field hides most of what the hospital chapter
  describes.
- Between
  `{python} number((100 * masking["Discharges with masked affiliation"] / masking["DEIS discharges (all establishments)"]).min(), 1)`%
  and
  `{python} number((100 * masking["Discharges with masked affiliation"] / masking["DEIS discharges (all establishments)"]).max(), 1)`%
  of DEIS discharges are published with establishment and sex masked — and, in
  `{python} year_span(age_masked_years)`, age as well — and the public network carries
  about `{python} number(split["Public share of principal F84 (%)"].iloc[-1], 0)`% of the principal-F84
  records against about `{python} number(split["Public share of all discharges (%)"].iloc[-1], 0)`% of all
  discharges — the gap that bounds what a public-hospital database can say about the country.
